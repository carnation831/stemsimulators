How Soap Works

Look at a soap molecule, test what mixes with water, and then wash a greasy spot. Soap works because one end is attracted to water and the other end mixes with oil.

1. Soap molecule explorer

Sodium stearate is one real soap, made from stearic acid. Bar soap is a mix of similar molecules. Every one of them has a long tail and a charged head.

Sodium stearate · CH3(CH2)16COONa

In water the sodium ion leaves: CH3(CH2)16COO− + Na+

Simplified model. The stick is the hydrocarbon tail. The circle is the charged head. Na⁺ is a separate ion, not stuck on by a covalent bond. On a small screen, use the buttons under the picture.

Skeletal formula. Each corner or end of the zigzag is a carbon atom, and hydrogens on those carbons are not drawn. The two lines up to oxygen are a double bond. Na⁺ sits nearby as its own ion. Swipe sideways on a small screen to follow the chain.

Two opposite ends

Select the head or the tail. Hover or tap the drawing, or use the buttons. The two ends behave in opposite ways around water.

2. Polar or nonpolar, soluble or insoluble

Water is the liquid in the beaker. Add salt, sugar, oil, or more water and watch what happens. The close-up shows the partial charges that decide the result.

Like dissolves like. Polar water dissolves polar and ionic substances. It does not dissolve nonpolar oil and grease.

Drag a substance into the beaker, or select it. On a keyboard, Tab to a substance and press Enter.

  • Bent shape = water
  • Circle = Na⁺
  • Square = Cl⁻
  • Hexagon = sugar
  • Gold layer = oil

    3. How soap cleans: micelles

    A greasy spot is stuck to cloth. Water alone leaves it there. Add soap, scrub so micelles can form, and rinse them away. The cross-section shows the same pattern up close: heads out, tails in.

    Micelle cross-section

    A slice through a micelle. Real micelles are tiny balls, far too small to see. This picture enlarges one. Circle heads face outward, toward water. Line tails face inward. Grease sits in the nonpolar center.

    • Circle = hydrophilic head (polar, charged)
    • Line = hydrophobic tail (nonpolar)
    • Gold = oil or grease
    • Bent molecule = water

    What is happening

    Water alone is in the basin, and the grease is still on the cloth. Grease is nonpolar and hydrophobic. Water is polar. Nothing in plain water can grab the grease, so the spot stays.

    4. Key ideas

    Short meanings for the words in this simulation. Each link jumps to the picture that shows it.

    Hydrophobic
    Water-fearing. The soap tail, oil, and grease are hydrophobic. They stay away from water. See the tail
    Hydrophilic
    Water-loving. The charged soap head is hydrophilic. It is attracted to water. See the head
    Polar
    Charge is uneven, often drawn as δ+ and δ−. Water is polar. The soap head is polar and ionic, which is a full charge rather than a partial one. See water
    Nonpolar
    Electrons are shared more evenly, so there are no charged ends. Oil, grease, and the soap tail are nonpolar. See oil
    Soluble
    Able to dissolve and spread through a liquid. Salt and sugar are soluble in water. See salt dissolve
    Insoluble
    Not able to dissolve. Oil is insoluble in water and stays as its own layer. Soap carries grease off inside micelles, but the grease itself is still insoluble. See oil separate
    Micelle
    A tiny cluster of soap. Hydrophilic heads point out toward water. Hydrophobic tails point in and hold grease in the middle. See micelles
    Surfactant
    A surface-active molecule, such as soap. One end is hydrophilic and the other is hydrophobic, so it can sit between oil and water. Surfactants also help water spread and wet a surface. See soap at the grease
