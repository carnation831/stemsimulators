How Soap Is Made

A fat or oil and a strong base turn into soap and glycerol. This is the reaction that makes the soap molecules from How Soap Works. It is a follow-on demo, not a scored activity.

Safety — virtual demo only

Lye is caustic. Sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide can burn skin and eyes. Real soap-making needs an adult, goggles, and gloves, and a careful recipe. Do not mix lye from this page. Nothing here is a set of instructions for a real batch.

Saponification in a pot

Add a triglyceride and lye water, stir them together, then heat. Hydroxide breaks the ester links. Each fatty-acid chain leaves as a soap molecule, and glycerol is left behind. Afterward, bar soap is cured and can be salted out of the glycerol water.

Key ideas

Short meanings for the words in this reaction.

Saponification
The reaction that makes soap. A triglyceride and a strong base become glycerol and three fatty-acid salts. See the pot
Triglyceride
A fat or oil molecule. One glycerol backbone holds three fatty-acid chains. The model animal fat is glyceryl tristearate, C57H110O6.
Ester
The link that joins each fatty acid to glycerol. The hydroxide ion breaks that link. A triglyceride has three esters, so it needs three hydroxide ions.
Base / lye
NaOH or KOH, dissolved in water. The hydroxide ion, OH⁻, does the chemistry. NaOH makes bar soap. KOH makes softer or liquid soap. Too much lye left over is caustic.
Glycerol
The small molecule left when the three chains break off, C3H5(OH)3. It is also called glycerin. It can stay in the soap or be separated by salting out.
Fatty acid salt
Soap. Sodium stearate is the sodium salt of stearic acid. Each one has a polar, charged head and a nonpolar tail. See how that molecule cleans
